# Motif semantics

Use motifs as contextual references, not generic proof that a page is Chinese.

## General method

Before using a motif, identify:

- period or date range;
- region or community when relevant;
- medium and object type;
- social, religious, ritual, court, commercial, or domestic setting;
- whether the exact form has rank, sacred, funerary, or institutional meaning.

## Motif guidance

| Motif | Safe modern use | Context to check |
|---|---|---|
| Cloud forms | Abstract dividers, borders, or low-contrast pattern | Shape and meaning vary greatly by period and medium |
| Meander or key pattern | Geometric border or structural rhythm | Avoid calling every angular repeat “traditional Chinese” without a source |
| Lotus | Botanical or Buddhist-associated reference | Religious context, period, region, and relationship to other Buddhist imagery |
| Apsara or flying figure | Museum, performance, or mural-inspired work with sources | Sacred imagery, cave and period specificity, costume and gesture |
| Dragon | Carefully sourced celebration, craft, architecture, or historical interpretation | Claw count, rank systems, garment and court regulation, period and object type |
| Phoenix or bird imagery | Celebration, craft, opera, wedding, or historical interpretation | Do not treat every bird as phoenix; check paired symbolism and rank context |
| Dragon and phoenix pair | Contemporary wedding or ceremonial reference when appropriate | Do not claim universal imperial exclusivity or universal marriage meaning across time |
| Bat | Auspicious wordplay and decorative-art reference | Confirm period, medium, and combination with other symbols |
| Shou character | Longevity context with readable and correct form | Do not distort the character or use it in unrelated funerary contexts |
| Eight Buddhist emblems | Explicitly contextualized Buddhist or historical material | Sacred meaning and tradition-specific use |
| Eight Immortals attributes | Daoist, opera, folk, or decorative-art context | Identify the exact objects and avoid mixing with Buddhist emblems |
| Waves and mountain forms | Textile, ceramics, stage, or landscape-inspired structure | Rank and court associations in specific combinations such as garment patterns |
| Floral scrolls | Low-contrast ornament derived from a specific object or medium | “Scrolling foliage” spans cultures and periods; document the source |

## Dragon and phoenix rule

Do not use the absolute statement “dragon and phoenix motifs are exclusive to royalty.” Their regulation and meaning depend on period, form, garment or object, rank, region, and setting. Some specific imperial combinations require caution, while dragon and phoenix imagery also appears in non-court and later popular contexts.

For historically specific work:

1. identify the object and date;
2. check whether the motif is rank-regulated or sacred;
3. preserve distinctive attributes rather than drawing a generic fantasy creature;
4. label modern simplification as interpretation.

## Decorative restraint

- Prefer one primary motif family and at most one supporting family per view.
- Keep culturally meaningful motifs out of error states, destructive actions, loading spinners, and arbitrary badges.
- Do not use a motif when typography, spacing, material color, or composition can communicate the intended mood more clearly.
- Avoid AI-generated pseudo-characters inside patterns.
